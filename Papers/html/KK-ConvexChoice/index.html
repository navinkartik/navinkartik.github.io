For multidimensional Euclidean type spaces, we study convex choice: from any choice set, the set of types that make the same choice is convex. We establish that, in a suitable sense, this property characterizes the sufficiency of local incentive constraints. Convex choice is also of interest more broadly, e.g., in cheap-talk games. We tie convex choice to a notion of directional single-crossing differences (DSCD). For an expected-utility agent choosing among lotteries, DSCD implies that preferences are either one-dimensional or must take the affine form that has been tractable in multidimensional mechanism design.

1 Introduction

A fundamental property in economic models with one-dimensional private information is the Spence-Mirrlees single-crossing property. Roughly equivalent to “interval choice”—the set of types for which an action is optimal is an interval—the single-crossing property has many uses. For example, it reduces incentive compatibility (IC) to local IC. Local IC requires only that “nearby” types have no incentive to mimic each other. Sufficiency of local IC is central to the tractability, and hence success, of influential paradigms like signaling and mechanism design.

Our paper is concerned with multidimensional environments. Consider an agent with utility function u⁢(a,θ), where a∈A is an allocation or choice variable and θ∈Θ is the agent’s type or preference parameter. Let Θ⊂ℝn be convex. We study a property we call convex choice: for any choice set B⊂A and any allocation a∈B, the set of types for which a is (uniquely) optimal is convex.111Our notion relates the preferences of different types. It is distinct from “convex preferences” in decision theory, which refers to the following property of a single preference relation: if a is preferred to b, then convex combinations of a and b (in some suitable sense) are also preferred to b.

We view convex choice as compelling. There is a sense in which it characterizes the sufficiency of local IC. More precisely, absent indifferences, convex choice characterizes when IC between any two types is equivalent to local IC along the line segment between those two types (Proposition 1). The necessity of convex choice for such “integration up” of local IC suggests that absent convex choice, a mechanism-design model is unlikely to be tractable.222We also discuss how sufficiency of local IC on the full type space is more or less characterized by a weaker property we call connected choice (Proposition 2); this property does not, however, deliver the tractability of integrating up local IC along line segments. Convex choice is relevant for other reasons as well. For instance, it delivers an extension of Saks and Yu’s (2005) result on the implementability of allocation rules in settings with transfers (Proposition 3). It also offers a natural generalization of “interval equilibria” in multidimensional versions of Crawford and Sobel’s (1982) famous cheap-talk model.

Proposition 4 shows that convex choice is essentially equivalent to a form of single crossing that we term directional single-crossing differences (DSCD). Modulo details, DSCD requires that for any two (undominated) allocations, the set of types that prefer one to the other is defined by a half-space. Importantly, the orientation of the half-space can vary with the allocation pair. With A,Θ⊂ℝ≥0n, leading examples of DSCD are the quadratic-loss utility u⁢(a,θ)=−‖a−θ‖2, where ∥⋅∥ is the Euclidean norm, and the constant-elasticity-of-substitution utility u⁢(a,θ)=(∑i=1n(ai)r⁢θi)s with parameters r,s>0.

Studies of multidimensional mechanism design frequently assume the affine form u⁢(a,θ)=v⁢(a)⋅θ+w⁢(a) (e.g., Armstrong, 1996; Rochet and Choné, 1998; Manelli and Vincent, 2007; Kleiner, 2026). Our approach delivers a novel perspective on this specification. Propositions 5 and 6 show that if the convex choice/DSCD requirement is strengthened to hold for expected utility over allocation lotteries, then—subject to some regularity conditions—either the setting is effectively one-dimensional, or preferences can be represented in the affine form. In particular, this provides a justification for the common focus on quadratic-loss utility: when preferences depend only on the distance between allocation and type, essentially only quadratic loss with a weighted Euclidean norm has DSCD over lotteries.

One interpretation of these results is that when allocation lotteries are important, multidimensional mechanism design is unlikely to be generally tractable beyond the affine form. By “tractable”, we mean finding a useful characterization of the set of IC mechanisms, which is the first step in the canonical Myersonian approach (Myerson, 1981), prior to optimization. Lotteries may be inescapable when stochastic mechanisms are allowed or when there are other agents and Bayesian incentive constraints are considered. On the flip side, a constructive interpretation of Propositions 5 and 6 is that they provide some guidance for when mechanism designers should focus on alternative, non-Myersonian, approaches; see, for example, Haghpanah and Hartline (2021) in the context of monopoly bundling. In this regard, our Proposition 3 on implementability of allocation rules may be useful for future work.

Related Literature.

As elaborated later, convex choice is closely related to notions in Grandmont (1978) and, in contemporaneous work to ours, Rodriguez (2026) and Wu and Wu (2026). In particular, Grandmont (1978) has a similar characterization to our Proposition 4. Beyond that, our emphasis and results are largely distinct: Grandmont (1978) and Wu and Wu (2026) focus on social choice, and Rodriguez (2026) on cheap talk; none of these papers relate convex choice to the sufficiency of local IC or implementability in mechanism design, or have any analog to our characterization of DSCD over lotteries.

Our work also relates to Carroll (2012), Kartik et al. (2024b), and McAfee and McMillan (1988). We detail these and some other connections subsequently.

2 Convex Choice and Applications

There is an agent with type θ∈Θ⊂ℝn, where Θ is convex. We write θ(i) for the i-th coordinate of θ, and θ>θ′ if θ(i)≥θ(i)′ for all i with strict inequality for some i. We denote the line segment between types θ′ and θ′′ by

ℓ⁢(θ′,θ′′):={θ:∃λ∈[0,1]⁢ with ⁢θ=λ⁢θ′+(1−λ)⁢θ′′}.

The agent takes an action, or chooses an allocation, a∈A, with preferences given by the utility function u:A×Θ→ℝ. We denote the set of types for whom action a is optimal from choice set B⊂A either strictly or weakly by333Throughout, the “⊂” symbol means “weak subset”.

SaB:={θ∈Θ:{a}=arg⁡maxa′∈B⁡u⁢(a′,θ)}andWaB:={θ∈Θ:a∈arg⁡maxa′∈B⁡u⁢(a′,θ)}.
Definition 1.

u has convex choice if for all finite B⊂A and a∈B, the set SaB is convex.

In other words, convex choice requires that from any finite choice set, the set of types that find an action uniquely optimal is convex. It is equivalent to only consider all binary choice sets, as the intersection of convex sets is convex. Convex choice implies that the preferences of any type θ∈ℓ⁢(θ′,θ′′) are “between” those of θ′ and θ′′, in the sense of Grandmont (1978).444Grandmont’s betweenness notion is stated for binary relations that need not be transitive; so, in his setting, choice from non-binary choice sets may not be well-defined. His notion also imposes some requirements concerning indifference. Ignoring indifferences and assuming transitivity, convex choice is equivalent to the preferences of any θ∈ℓ⁢(θ′,θ′′) being between those of θ′ and θ′′. Convex choice is also very similar to Rodriguez’s (2026) “convex-regular property”; the notions are equivalent absent indifferences.

Another related, but more substantially different, notion to convex choice is Kartik et al.’s (2024b) “interval choice”. They require that if θ′′>θ′>θ and some action is optimal for θ and θ′′, then it is also optimal for θ′. (Those authors use weak optimality whereas we use strict optimality; this difference is minor.) Absent indifferences, convex and interval choice are equivalent when Θ⊂ℝ, but they are incomparable when Θ is multidimensional (Example B.1). Convex sets are, of course, a generalization of one-dimensional intervals to multiple dimensions. We will discuss yet another alternative, connected sets, subsequently.

The interpretation of convex choice in any context depends on what the agent’s type represents. Although we are formally studying a single-agent choice problem, we view the agent’s type as having external economic meaning beyond just parameterizing the agent’s preferences. For instance, the type could represent the agent’s information, ability, or ideology on multiple dimensions, which is relevant to an observer, principal, or politician. We thus take the type space as given, and do not consider reparameterizing it.

2.1 Sufficiency of Local Incentive Constraints

A central question in mechanism design, and information economics more broadly, is when local incentive constraints ensure global ones. To define our notions of incentive compatibility, let Nθ⊂Θ denote an open neighborhood—simply neighborhood hereafter—of type θ in the relative topology of Θ. Throughout, we focus on direct mechanisms Θ→A; stochastic mechanisms are subsumed by taking A to be a lottery space.

Definition 2.

A mechanism m:Θ→A is

  1. 1.

    incentive compatible (IC) if u⁢(m⁢(θ),θ)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ′),θ) for all θ,θ′∈Θ;

  2. 2.

    locally IC if for each θ∈Θ there is a neighborhood Nθ⊂Θ such that

    ∀θ′∈Nθ:u⁢(m⁢(θ),θ)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ′),θ)⁢ and ⁢u⁢(m⁢(θ′),θ′)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ),θ′). (LIC)

We will also refer to (local) IC of mechanisms defined on a subset of the type space Θ′⊂Θ; that simply refers to Definition 2 with Θ′ in place of Θ.

IC is a standard and fundamental property. Our formulation of local IC follows Carroll (2012): for every type θ, there is a set of nearby types for which (i) θ cannot profitably mimic those types and (ii) those types cannot profitably mimic θ. Requirement (ii) here is due to the non-discrete type space, and cannot be dispensed with.555Here is an example. Θ=[0,1], A={a′,a′′}, u⁢(a′,⋅)<u⁢(a′′,⋅), and m⁢(θ)=a′ if and only if θ<1/2. For every type θ<1/2, there is ε>0 such that all types in [0,θ+ε) receive the same allocation a′; every type θ≥1/2 receives its preferred allocation a′′. Hence, every θ has a neighborhood containing no type that θ would profitably mimic. Local IC fails, however, because any neighborhood of type 1/2 contains some types strictly below, all of which would profitably mimic 1/2. If we did not rule this out in local IC, then the example would contradict Proposition 1. Carroll (2012, p. 669) makes an analogous point in his framework. When Θ⊂ℝn, it is less demanding than imposing only requirement (i) but requiring that all neighborhoods Nθ in (LIC) have “size” bounded away from zero.666That is, there is ε>0 such that for all θ∈Θ, the ε-ball {θ′∈Θ:∥θ−θ′∥<ε} is contained in Nθ, where ∥⋅∥ is the Euclidean norm.

Plainly, IC implies local IC (because Θ is a neighborhood of every type). The converse is not true:

Example 1.

Θ=[0,1], A={a′,a′′}; types in (1/3,2/3) strictly prefer a′′ while all other types strictly prefer a′. Note that convex choice fails. The mechanism m⁢(θ)=a′ if θ≤1/3 and m⁢(θ)=a′′ if θ>1/3 is not IC. But it is locally IC: for every θ≠1/3, there is a neighborhood of θ on which m⁢(⋅) is constant; whereas for type 1/3, neither does it prefer to mimic any type nor does any nearby type prefer to mimic it.

We will see that, in a suitable sense, convex choice is necessary for local IC to imply IC. While it is also sufficient when preferences are strict, the next example shows that indifferences are a threat. In the example, preferences are continuous, the type space is one-dimensional, and the strict-preference regions are convex.

Example 2.

Θ=[0,1], A={a′,a′′}; types in [0,12) strictly prefer a′, types in (12,1) strictly prefer a′′, and types 12 and 1 are indifferent. The mechanism m⁢(1)=a′ and m⁢(θ)=a′′ for θ<1 is locally IC: type 1 is indifferent between the two actions, and every other type lies in the open set [0,1) on which m⁢(⋅) is constant. But it is not IC: every type below 12 strictly prefers reporting 1.

The problem in Example 2 is the indifference at θ=1, which cannot be approached by types that strictly prefer a′. The next definition rules out such indifferences.

Definition 3.

u has segment-thin indifferences if for all a′,a′′∈A and θ′,θ′′∈Θ, u⁢(a′,θ′)>u⁢(a′′,θ′)⁢ and ⁢u⁢(a′,θ′′)=u⁢(a′′,θ′′) imply there is a sequence {θn}→θ′′ such that θn∈ℓ⁢(θ′,θ′′) and u⁢(a′,θn)>u⁢(a′′,θn) for all n.

An implication of segment-thin indifferences is that for any two actions, if some type strictly prefers one of the actions then the set of types that are indifferent has empty interior. Given convex choice, segment-thin indifferences is a weak requirement; in particular, it trivially holds when there are no indifferences. The conjunction of convex choice and segment-thin indifferences is equivalent to: for all θ,θ′∈Θ and all a,a′∈A, if u⁢(a,θ)>u⁢(a′,θ) and u⁢(a,θ′)≥u⁢(a′,θ′), then u⁢(a,λ⁢θ+(1−λ)⁢θ′)>u⁢(a′,λ⁢θ+(1−λ)⁢θ′) for all λ∈(0,1).

We can now state our first main result.

Proposition 1.

The following are equivalent:

  1. 1.

    u has convex choice and segment-thin indifferences;

  2. 2.

    for any line segment ℓ⊂Θ and any mechanism m:ℓ→A, if m is locally IC then it is IC.

(All proofs are in the appendices.)

The proposition says that convex choice and segment-thin indifferences jointly characterize when IC between any two types θ and θ′ can be determined by just checking local IC along the line segment ℓ⁢(θ,θ′). Such “integration up” is a common strategy used to verify IC.

Examples 1 and 2 are illustrative of the general argument for why both convex choice and segment-thin indifferences are necessary for Proposition 1’s second statement. Here is a heuristic sketch for why convex choice is sufficient when there are no indifferences. Take any θ and θ′. Consider a fine grid of types θ=θ1,…,θK=θ′ traversing the line segment ℓ⁢(θ,θ′). We can regard local IC as implying u⁢(m⁢(θ1),θ1)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ2),θ1), u⁢(m⁢(θ2),θ2)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ3),θ2), and u⁢(m⁢(θ3),θ3)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ2),θ3). Absent indifferences, all those inequalities are strict when the compared allocations differ, as we suppose for this sketch. Convex choice then implies u⁢(m⁢(θ1),θ1)>u⁢(m⁢(θ3),θ1); otherwise, both θ1 and θ3 strictly (absent indifferences) prefer m⁢(θ3) to m⁢(θ2), hence so must θ2, a contradiction. Iterating this logic, using the combination of local IC and convex choice each time, yields u⁢(m⁢(θ1),θ1)>u⁢(m⁢(θi),θ1) for all i=2,…,K. Consequently, u⁢(m⁢(θ),θ)>u⁢(m⁢(θ′),θ).

Local IC on the full type space implies local IC on every line segment.777Formally, if m:Θ→A is locally IC, then for every line segment ℓ⊂Θ, the mechanism m′:ℓ→A defined by m′⁢(θ):=m⁢(θ) is locally IC (on ℓ). Hence, Proposition 1 implies that convex choice, together with segment-thin indifferences, ensures sufficiency of local IC on the full type space. But sufficiency on the full type space does not require convex choice, even assuming segment-thin (or no) indifferences. What matters instead is that from any finite choice set, the set of types that find an action uniquely optimal is connected.888Recall that a set is connected if it cannot be partitioned into two nonempty separated sets, i.e., sets neither of which intersects the closure of the other.

Definition 4.

u has connected choice if for all finite B⊂A and a∈B, the set SaB is connected.

As convex sets are connected, connected choice is weaker than convex choice; indeed, convex choice is connected choice imposed on every line segment, and the notions are equivalent in one dimension.

Our characterization on the full type space also replaces segment-thin indifferences, which is formulated for line segments, with a sister condition. Let clΘ denote the closure in Θ.

Definition 5.

u has thin indifferences if for all finite B⊂A and a∈B,

SaB≠∅⟹WaB⊂clΘ⁡SaB.

In words, thin indifferences says that from any finite choice set in which some action is uniquely optimal for some type, every type for which that action is optimal is a limit of types for which it is uniquely optimal.

Proposition 2.

Assume that for all a,a′∈A, the set {θ∈Θ:u⁢(a,θ)>u⁢(a′,θ)} is open. The following are equivalent:

  1. 1.

    u has connected choice and thin indifferences;

  2. 2.

    every locally IC mechanism with finite range is IC.

Proposition 2 holds verbatim, with the same proof, on any connected topological type space. The intuition for sufficiency of connected choice is as follows. Take a locally IC mechanism m with finite range, fix an action a in that range, and consider the types Sam⁢(Θ) for which a is uniquely optimal in the range. By local IC, these types must receive a if they are near a type that receives a. As connected choice makes Sam⁢(Θ) connected, either all these types receive a or none does. In other words, a type cannot be denied its uniquely optimal action in the mechanism’s range if that action goes to another type that also finds it uniquely optimal. The formal proof extends this argument to show there cannot be any violation of IC, using thin indifferences to deal with types whose optimal action is not unique. In the other direction, a violation of either connected choice or thin indifferences yields a locally IC mechanism that is not IC, in the spirit of Examples 1 and 2.

The openness assumption in Proposition 2 holds, in particular, when u⁢(a,⋅) is continuous for every a; unlike continuity, the assumption is ordinal, being preserved under any utility representation. The assumption plays no role in the necessity of connected choice or thin indifferences;999When Θ is an arbitrary topological space, this holds provided that all singleton type sets are closed. it can also be dropped for sufficiency of the conditions if thin indifferences is strengthened as indicated in the proposition’s proof.

Remark 1.

Segment-thin indifferences is thin indifferences for all binary choice sets, imposed on every line segment; given convex choice, the restriction to binary choice sets is without loss. In general, neither segment-thin indifferences nor thin indifferences implies the other. However, under the openness assumption of Proposition 2, segment-thin indifferences implies thin indifferences. These claims are proved in Appendix A.3.

Proposition 2 notwithstanding, we view the sufficiency of local IC on the full type space as generally inadequate to render multidimensional incentive constraints tractable. In particular, it seems intractable to verify IC between two types by checking local IC along (the typically uncountable set of) all paths between those types. Instead, tractability does obtain if IC can be verified via local IC along their line segment; that is a one-dimensional task. Hence our interest in the more demanding form of sufficiency of local IC in Proposition 1. Let us now compare our approach with that of Carroll (2012) in his “cardinal type space” analysis. We view the approaches as complementary: Carroll equates types with preferences (more precisely, utilities) and studies properties of the type space that guarantee sufficiency of local IC; instead, we fix an abstract (convex Euclidean) type space and study properties of the utility function. As previously noted, types may have external meaning beyond the agent’s preferences. But even through the narrow lens of preferences, Berger et al. (2017, p. 369) provide an insightful discussion of “parameter representations”, like ours, versus “domain representations”, like Carroll’s. We add that the flexibility in choosing a parameter representation (the type space and the utility function) has advantages and disadvantages, roughly corresponding to whether one relies on the necessity or the sufficiency of convex choice in Proposition 1. We think the latter is more important, and hence the flexibility is more of an advantage.

Mathematically, Proposition 1 subsumes Carroll’s (2012) Proposition 1. To see why, note that Carroll assumes each a∈A⊂ℝn is a probability vector over a finite set of n outcomes, and he associates types with their utility vectors over those outcomes. We nest his framework by setting u⁢(a,θ)=a⋅θ. Our notion of local IC (and IC) is then equivalent to his. Carroll’s Proposition 1 says that given convexity of Θ, local IC implies IC. The conclusion follows from our Proposition 1 because u in this specification has convex choice and segment-thin indifferences.

Our result is more general than Carroll’s (2012). First, we do not require finitely many outcomes. Second, and more substantively, there are utility functions that satisfy the conditions of Proposition 1 but preclude obtaining a convex type space by identifying types with utility vectors over outcomes (so Carroll’s result has no bite). For example, let X={0,1/2,1} be a set of three outcomes, A be the lotteries over X, and for Θ=[0,1] let u⁢(a,θ)=∑x∈Xax⁢(−|x−θ|), i.e., expected utility with a linear-loss Bernoulli utility. As verified in Example B.2, this specification has convex choice and segment-thin indifferences, yet because preferences over lotteries pin down each type’s utility vector over X up to a positive affine transformation, no choice of these utility vectors yields a convex set.

So far we have emphasized the sufficiency of local IC. But convex choice is relevant and useful more broadly. We next discuss two other applications.

2.2 Implementability

In mechanism design with transfers, an important question is whether a given allocation rule is implementable. Formally, let A≡Y×ℝ, where Y is a “physical” allocation space and ℝ is the space of transfers. Writing a≡(y,t), assume u⁢((y,t),θ)=u~⁢(y,θ)−t for some function u~. An allocation rule υ:Θ→Y is implementable if there exists a transfer rule τ:Θ→ℝ such that m≡(υ,τ) is IC. We restrict attention to allocation rules with finite range. Rochet (1987) establishes that an allocation rule is implementable if and only if it is “cyclically monotone”. A less demanding condition, which only considers pairs of types, is that of weak monotonicity:

∀θ,θ′∈Θ:u~⁢(υ⁢(θ),θ)−u~⁢(υ⁢(θ′),θ)≥u~⁢(υ⁢(θ),θ′)−u~⁢(υ⁢(θ′),θ′).

It is straightforward that weak monotonicity is necessary for implementability. For Y⊂ℝn with dot product valuations, u~⁢(y,θ)=y⋅θ, Saks and Yu (2005) establish that if Θ is convex, then weak monotonicity is also sufficient for implementability; see Bikhchandani et al. (2006) and Archer and Kleinberg (2014) as well. Building on Berger et al. (2017, Theorem 5), convex choice yields the following generalization, which additionally does not require Y⊂ℝn.

Proposition 3.

Assume u⁢((y,t),θ)=u~⁢(y,θ)−t has convex choice and is continuous in θ. If an allocation rule with finite range is weakly monotone, then it is implementable.

To interpret the result, note that in this setting with transfers, convex choice holds if and only if for all y,y′∈Y, the difference u~⁢(y,⋅)−u~⁢(y′,⋅) is monotonic along every line segment. For, the types that strictly prefer (y,t) to (y′,t′) are those with u~⁢(y,θ)−u~⁢(y′,θ)>t−t′, and t−t′ is arbitrary. Proposition 3 thus extends to multiple dimensions the familiar one-dimensional result that if u~ has increasing differences for a suitable ordering of Y, then weak monotonicity implies implementability.

2.3 Cheap Talk

In cheap-talk or costly-signaling applications, it is natural to ask whether, in equilibrium, the set of types that choose a particular signal is convex. Modulo details about indifferences, the key condition to guarantee that all equilibria have this “convex partitional” property is that the sender’s utility have convex choice.

Indeed, there has been interest in extending Crawford and Sobel’s (1982) classic result on interval equilibria in one-dimensional cheap talk to multiple dimensions. In a working paper version of Levy and Razin (2007), Levy and Razin (2004) assume Θ=A=ℝ2 and sender utility u⁢(a,θ)=−(∑i∈{1,2}α(i)⁢|a(i)−(b(i)+θ(i))|p)1p with parameters α∈ℝ>02, b∈ℝ2, and p>1. It can be verified that this specification has convex choice if and only if p=2 (cf. Remark 2 in the next section). Consistent with that, Levy and Razin (2004) do not show that all equilibria are convex partitional; rather, in their Section 4.1, they identify parameters under which they can construct some such equilibria.121212In other working paper versions, the authors assume p=2 and show that in this case all equilibria are convex partitional. Similarly, the class of sender utilities in Chakraborty and Harbaugh (2007) does not assure convex choice, and they only show existence of “comparative cheap-talk” equilibria that are convex partitional.

Also germane are a few papers on common-interest cheap-talk games with an infinite type space but a finite message space. Jäger et al. (2011) posit u⁢(a,θ)=−l⁢(‖a−θ‖) for an arbitrary norm ∥⋅∥ and strictly increasing function l⁢(⋅), but in their Corollary 1 on optimal equilibria being convex partitional (modulo indifferences—which we ignore in the rest of this paragraph), they assume the Euclidean norm, which guarantees convex choice. They note that other norms can be problematic; we will see that weighted Euclidean norms would also work, but none others (Remark 2).131313Strictly speaking, Jäger et al.’s (2011) Example 1/Figure 1 with the maximum norm does not violate convex choice—rather, the issue there is about indifferences—but perturbations of their example’s actions would illustrate a failure of convex choice. Saint-Paul (2017, Theorem 2) establishes that optimal equilibria are convex partitional under a weaker condition than Jäger et al. (2011). Saint-Paul’s condition implies the directional single-crossing property discussed in the next section, which we show essentially characterizes convex choice (Proposition 4). Sobel (2016, Proposition 3) extends Saint-Paul’s result by directly assuming convex choice. Finally, Rodriguez (2026, Proposition 2) shows that when there are multiple senders with common information, message profiles in efficient equilibria have “convex meaning”.

3 Directional Single Crossing

To unpack which preferences have convex choice, we next develop a connection with single crossing.

Definition 6.

A function f:Θ→ℝ is directionally single crossing (DSC) if there is α∈ℝn∖{0} such that for all θ,θ′∈Θ,

(θ−θ′)⋅α≥0⟹sign⁡(f⁢(θ))≥sign⁡(f⁢(θ′)).

DSC says that for any θ and θ′ such that θ is to the right of the hyperplane passing through θ′ in the direction of α (i.e., with normal vector α), we have sign⁡(f⁢(θ))≥sign⁡(f⁢(θ′)). Hence, we will sometimes say more explicitly that a function is DSC in the direction α. DSC is equivalent to the sets {θ:f⁢(θ)<0} and {θ:f⁢(θ)>0} being either empty, full (i.e., all of Θ), or intersections of Θ with half-spaces defined by parallel (possibly identical) hyperplanes. See 2(a): the left panel depicts a typical DSC function with a “thin” zero set, illustrating the geometry of the definition; the middle panel depicts a DSC function with a “thick” zero set; and the right panel depicts a violation of DSC. Note that DSC implies that along any line segment, the sign of the function is monotonic.

Definition 7.

The utility function u has directionally single-crossing differences (DSCD) if for all a,a′∈A, the difference Da,a′⁢(θ):=u⁢(a,θ)−u⁢(a′,θ) is DSC.

DSCD can be viewed as saying that for any pair of actions a and a′, the strict-preference sets {θ:u⁢(a,θ)>u⁢(a′,θ)} and {θ:u⁢(a,θ)<u⁢(a′,θ)} are parallel half-spaces, each either open or closed, intersected with the type space. Importantly, the direction of the hyperplanes defining these half-spaces can vary across action pairs.

Here are two leading families of DSCD when A⊂ℝn: (i) weighted Euclidean preferences, where u⁢(a,θ) is any strictly decreasing function of (a−θ)⁢W⁢(a−θ)T with W an n×n symmetric positive definite matrix; and (ii) constant-elasticity-of-substitution (CES) preferences, where A,Θ⊂ℝ≥0n and u⁢(a,θ)=(∑i=1n(a(i))r⁢θ(i))s with parameters r>0 and s>0.141414The superscript T denotes transposition. Family (i) satisfies DSCD with the direction α=W⁢(a−a′)T and family (ii) with α=ar−(a′)r. Both families also have segment-thin indifferences. Adding an arbitrary type-independent function w⁢(a) preserves DSCD in family (i) when u⁢(a,θ) is linear in (a−θ)⁢W⁢(a−θ)T and in family (ii) when s=1, because in both cases pairwise utility differences are affine in type. The latter includes u⁢(a,θ)=a⋅θ+w⁢(a), which is frequently used in multidimensional mechanism design and which we will return to.

Remark 2.

Consider norm-based preferences, where A⊂ℝn and u⁢(a,θ)=−l⁢(∥a−θ∥) for a strictly increasing function l⁢(⋅) and some norm ∥⋅∥ on ℝn. If the norm is weighted Euclidean (i.e., there is a symmetric positive definite n×n matrix W such that ∥x∥=x⁢W⁢xT for all x∈ℝn), then we have weighted Euclidean preferences and hence DSCD. Conversely, if the norm is not weighted Euclidean, then DSCD is violated if A∩Θ has nonempty interior; Appendix A.5 provides a proof.

The following result shows that DSCD “almost” characterizes convex choice. Loosely speaking, u strictly violates DSCD if for some pair of actions, the strict preference between them switches more than once in every direction; the formal definition is deferred to Appendix A.6.

Proposition 4.

If u has DSCD, then u has convex choice. If u has convex choice, then it does not strictly violate DSCD.

In light of its half-spaces interpretation, DSCD implying convex choice is straightforward. Conversely, a separating hyperplane theorem yields that under convex choice there is no strict violation of DSCD (although DSCD itself can fail; see Example B.3).

Proposition 4 is closely related to a result of Grandmont (1978, p. 322). Grandmont’s condition (H.2) is a little stronger than imposing both convex choice and segment-thin indifferences. Combined with a continuity condition—Grandmont’s (H.1)—and an assumption that the convex type space is open, he obtains a characterization that is similar to, but more restrictive than, DSCD. See Appendix C.1 for details. The leading families of DSCD given above satisfy Grandmont’s characterization. A one-dimensional example of DSCD that does not is Θ=(0,1), A={a′,a′′}, u⁢(a′,⋅)=0, u⁢(a′′,θ)=−1 if θ<1/2 and u⁢(a′′,θ)=1 if θ≥1/2; Grandmont’s continuity requirement fails here.

If Θ⊂ℝ, then DSCD is equivalent to Kartik et al.’s (2024b) “single-crossing differences” and, modulo indifferences, Proposition 4 is equivalent to part 1 of their Theorem 1. But in multiple dimensions the notions are distinct, and their result is not about convex choice. Similarly, Milgrom and Shannon’s (1994) single-crossing preferences do not guarantee convex choice when Θ is multidimensional.151515Neither does Barthel and Sabarwal’s (2018) “i-single crossing property”. Their notion is motivated by Quah’s (2007) weakening of Milgrom and Shannon’s (1994) comparisons of constraint/choice sets. Convex choice considers all choice sets.

McAfee and McMillan (1988) present a notion of “generalized single crossing”. Their condition is formulated for a Euclidean allocation space and twice-differentiable utilities. For differentiable mechanisms, they establish that their condition implies that checking local IC along line segments is sufficient. Appendix C.2 provides an example demonstrating that McAfee and McMillan’s condition does not imply convex choice, and hence does not imply DSCD. Consequently, as displayed explicitly in our example, there are non-differentiable mechanisms for which their condition does not yield sufficiency of local IC.

3.1 Convex Environments

In various settings, the agent may be choosing among lotteries. For instance, a mechanism designer may consider stochastic mechanisms; indeed, the revelation principle in general requires allowing for those. Or, the mechanism may take inputs from other agents with private information, so that from the interim point of view of one agent, her report induces a lottery. Alternatively, in a cheap-talk application, the receiver’s decision may be determined by both the sender’s message and the receiver’s private preference type; Kartik et al. (2024b) detail this application with Θ,A⊂ℝ.

Following Kartik et al. (2024b), we say that the environment (A,Θ,u) is convex if

 the set of functions ⁢{u⁢(a,⋅):Θ→ℝ}a∈A⁢ is convex. (⋆)

That is, a convex environment has the property that for any pair of actions and any weighting, there is a third action that replicates the weighted sum of utilities of the original actions. Note that convexity is a property of the utility function rather than of A. However, if A is convex, then it is straightforward that (⋆) is assured by linearity of u in its first argument. Expected utility over a convex set of lotteries (e.g., all lotteries) is thus a convex environment. In fact, rank-dependent expected utility also produces a convex environment (Kartik et al., 2024b, Example 2). An example without lotteries is A=[a¯1,a¯1]×…×[a¯k,a¯k]⊂ℝk and u⁢(a,θ)=∑i=1kgi⁢(a(i))⁢fi⁢(θ) with arbitrary functions (fi)i=1k and continuous functions (gi)i=1k.

The following concepts are useful to elucidate the implications of DSCD in convex environments. We say that the utility function u~⁢(a,θ) represents u⁢(a,θ) if u~⁢(a,θ)=h⁢(u⁢(a,θ),θ) for some function h such that for all θ, h⁢(⋅,θ):ℝ→ℝ is strictly increasing. This is simply the standard notion of (type-dependent) preference representation. An affine representation is any representation that is affine in θ, i.e., has the form v⁢(a)⋅θ+w⁢(a) for some v:A→ℝn and w:A→ℝ. We say that u is one-dimensional if there are α∈ℝn∖{0} and u~:A×ℝ→ℝ such that u⁢(a,θ)≥(>)⁢u⁢(a′,θ) if and only if u~⁢(a,α⋅θ)≥(>)⁢u~⁢(a′,α⋅θ).161616For a DSCD preference, this is equivalent to the utility difference between every pair of actions satisfying DSC in a direction parallel to α. In other words, any type θ’s preferences are fully determined by the one-dimensional sufficient statistic α⋅θ. A type θ is totally indifferent if u⁢(a,θ)=u⁢(a′,θ) for all a,a′∈A.

It bears emphasis that our notion of representation does not permit redefining types, only the utility function. If types could be redefined, including altering the type space, then expected utility over lotteries with a finite number of outcomes would always have an affine representation, simply by viewing each type as the vector of its utilities over outcomes. This is not true once types are fixed (Example B.4).

Regardless of a convex environment, any utility function with an affine representation satisfies DSCD. For, any affine utility clearly satisfies DSCD, and DSCD is an ordinal property that is preserved by any representation. If preferences are one-dimensional, then even in a convex environment there are DSCD utilities that do not have an affine representation (Example B.4). The following result says that if preferences are not one-dimensional, then under some “regularity” conditions, DSCD in a convex environment is in fact characterized by an affine utility representation.

Proposition 5.

Assume Θ=ℝn, u⁢(a,θ) is differentiable in θ, and no type is totally indifferent.171717As explained in the proof, instead of no totally indifferent type, it is enough to assume that for any type θ that is totally indifferent, there are two actions a′ and a′′ such that ∇[u⁢(a′,θ)−u⁢(a′′,θ)]≠0. If the environment is convex and u is not one-dimensional, then u has DSCD if and only if it has an affine representation.

Proof idea.

We pick an arbitrary action a∗ and normalize u⁢(a∗,⋅)=0. DSCD and the convex environment then imply that every linear combination of the functions u⁢(a,⋅), a∈A, is DSC.

Suppose that preferences are not one-dimensional. Then there are two such combinations, u1 and u2, whose DSC directions cannot be chosen to be parallel, so their zero sets contain non-parallel hyperplanes. Since Θ=ℝn, these hyperplanes intersect, and so u1 and u2 vanish at some common type θ0. For simplicity, suppose also that ∇u1⁢(θ0)≠0; the formal proof shows how to arrange this, using that no type is totally indifferent.

The key observation is that a DSC function that vanishes at two types vanishes on the line segment between them. Fix any θ1∈Θ. The combination u2⁢(θ1)⁢u1−u1⁢(θ1)⁢u2 is DSC and vanishes at θ0 and θ1, so its derivative at θ0 in the direction θ1−θ0 is zero: writing Li⁢(θ):=∇ui⁢(θ0)⋅(θ−θ0) for i=1,2,

u2⁢(θ1)⁢L1⁢(θ1)=u1⁢(θ1)⁢L2⁢(θ1).

We now multiply all utilities by L1⁢(θ)/u1⁢(θ). DSC and ∇u1⁢(θ0)≠0 ensure that this factor is positive wherever it is defined, so the rescaled utilities still represent u. The rescaled u1 is L1 and, by the preceding display, the rescaled u2 is L2; both are affine.

Finally, choose a type where u2 vanishes but u1 does not. From any other utility u⁢(a,⋅), subtract a multiple of u1 so that the difference also vanishes at this type. Repeating the argument above at this type, with u2 in place of u1, we can show that the rescaled difference is affine, which implies that the rescaled u⁢(a,⋅) is affine. The formal proof verifies that the rescaling can also be defined on the zero set of u1. ∎

Recall that DSCD essentially characterizes convex choice (Proposition 4), and we have argued that convex choice is crucial for tractability (Proposition 1) and appealing for other reasons (e.g., in cheap-talk games). Moreover, in many problems the set of lotteries to consider cannot be substantially restricted; this can arise with stochastic mechanisms, or with multiple agents and deterministic mechanisms, where each agent’s Bayesian incentive constraints involve allocation lotteries induced by the other agents’ types. We thus view Proposition 5 as suggesting that in many settings, genuinely multidimensional expected-utility preferences without an affine representation will be unwieldy. This may shed light, in particular, on the prominence of the affine form in multidimensional mechanism design.181818Some work with a multidimensional type space and non-affine utilities effectively assumes preferences are one-dimensional (e.g., Ghili, 2023). It is generally difficult in those problems to rule out optimality of stochastic mechanisms, even under the affine form; see, e.g., Pycia (2006) and Manelli and Vincent (2007). By contrast, there are canonical one-dimensional settings in which “regularity” assumptions do ensure that deterministic mechanisms are optimal (Strausz, 2006); in these cases, solutions have been obtained assuming only DSCD over deterministic allocations.191919Strausz’s (2006) result is for a single-agent setting with transfers and quasi-linear utility. Beyond that, even one-dimensional mechanism design frequently assumes functional forms that assure DSCD over lotteries; Remark B.1 details the relevant restrictions. Examples in the context of delegation include Amador and Bagwell (2020) and Kartik et al. (2021).

Example B.6 shows that the assumption of Θ=ℝn cannot be dropped from Proposition 5. But one can obtain the result with alternative assumptions; what is important is some richness, beyond the convexity requirement (⋆), in terms of the family {u⁢(a,⋅)}a∈A when preferences are not one-dimensional. We offer one such variant below. Say that an action a∈A strictly dominates a′∈A if u⁢(a,θ)>u⁢(a′,θ) for all θ∈Θ. We also say that u is minimally rich if there are no actions a0,a1,a2∈A and functions λ0,λ1:A→ℝ such that for all a∈A and θ∈Θ,

u⁢(a,θ)−u⁢(a0,θ)=λ0⁢(a)⁢[u⁢(a1,θ)−u⁢(a0,θ)]+λ1⁢(a)⁢[u⁢(a2,θ)−u⁢(a0,θ)].

That is, minimal richness fails if, after normalizing u⁢(a0,⋅)=0, every action a’s utility u⁢(a,⋅) is a linear combination of u⁢(a1,⋅) and u⁢(a2,⋅).

Proposition 6.

Assume u⁢(a,θ) is differentiable in θ, minimally rich, and there is an action that strictly dominates another. If the environment is convex, then u has DSCD if and only if it has an affine representation.

Proposition 6 substitutes Proposition 5’s assumptions of Θ=ℝn and no total indifference with minimal richness and strict dominance between some pair of actions.202020Proposition 5’s assumption that u is not one-dimensional is implied by minimal richness and DSCD in a convex environment. The dominance assumption is satisfied whenever there is one component of the action space over which all types have strictly monotonic preferences; in particular, it holds in the quasi-linear environments common in mechanism design.

The “one-dimensional-or-affine” message of Propositions 5 and 6 can be illustrated with our leading DSCD families. First consider X,Θ⊂ℝ≥0n each with nonempty interior, and the CES utility u¯⁢(x,θ)=(∑i=1n(x(i))r⁢θ(i))s with r>0 and s>0. Although u¯ satisfies DSCD, does the induced expected-utility function u⁢(a,θ) over the lottery space A=Δ⁢X? In one dimension, n=1, yes: in that case any linear combination λ⁢u¯⁢(x,θ)+λ′⁢u¯⁢(x′,θ)=(λ⁢xr⁢s+λ′⁢(x′)r⁢s)⁢θs is monotonic in θ, hence (directionally) single crossing. But in multiple dimensions, n>1, u has DSCD if and only if s=1; indeed, for s≠1 even convex choice fails.212121Choose x0 and θ0 in the interiors of X and Θ, and a vector d≠0 with d⋅θ0=0. For sufficiently small ε,t>0, the outcomes x±:=(x0r±ε⁢d)1/r belong to X and the types θ±:=θ0±t⁢d belong to Θ. Let a be the equiprobable lottery on x+ and x−, and a′ the sure outcome x0. Type θ0 is indifferent, since x±r⋅θ0=x0r⋅θ0. At θ=θ±, the numbers x+r⋅θ and x−r⋅θ are distinct and average to x0r⋅θ, so Jensen’s inequality implies that both θ+ and θ− strictly prefer a to a′ if s>1, and a′ to a if s<1. Since θ0 is the midpoint of θ+ and θ−, convex choice is violated. As already mentioned, s=1 is the affine case prominent in multidimensional mechanism design.

Furthermore, when s=1, adding a function of the outcome alone keeps u¯ affine in type. Since adding a function of type alone does not change preferences, the case of s=1 thereby subsumes quadratic-loss preferences, u¯⁢(x,θ)=−‖x−θ‖2 with ∥⋅∥ the Euclidean norm. Interestingly, in his study of common-interest cheap talk with noisy communication—so that the sender’s message induces a lottery over receiver decisions—Bauch (2024, Proposition 6.3) requires quadratic loss to obtain a convex-partitional equilibrium result. Without noise, Jäger et al. (2011, Corollary 1) could allow for preferences based on more general functions of the Euclidean norm.222222Cheap-talk aficionados may note that Blume et al. (2007) get interval equilibria in their one-dimensional model of noisy communication without imposing quadratic loss on the sender’s utility. That owes to their special “truth-or-noise” garbling: either the sender’s message goes through correctly, or a random message is drawn from an exogenous distribution. So when choosing her message, the sender can condition on the no-noise event, effectively choosing among deterministic receiver actions. In a multidimensional setting, including Bauch’s (2024), truth-or-noise garbling would ensure that all equilibria are convex partitional (modulo details about indifferences) if the sender’s utility has DSCD, even if it does not have DSCD over lotteries. Our results shed light on this contrast: weighted Euclidean preferences have DSCD, but when n≥2 and Θ=ℝn, Remark A.1 shows that norm-based preferences have DSCD over lotteries if and only if the loss is quadratic in a weighted Euclidean norm.

Remark 3.

For convex environments, Kartik et al. (2024b) provide a characterization of preferences that satisfy their single-crossing differences (SCD). As Example B.5 shows, our message that multidimensional preferences must have an affine representation does not obtain under SCD, because SCD corresponds to interval choice whereas DSCD to convex choice.

4 Conclusion

Convex choice is a valuable property. We have shown that (i) subject to a mild condition on indifferences, it characterizes when local IC is sufficient on all line segments (Proposition 1), (ii) it simplifies verifying the implementability of allocation rules (Proposition 3) and is relevant to applications like cheap talk, (iii) it is essentially equivalent to a form of single crossing with a simple geometric interpretation (Proposition 4), and (iv) in convex environments satisfying some regularity conditions, it reduces to “one-dimensional or affine representation” (Propositions 5 and 6). Convex choice has also been used in other contexts, such as preference aggregation (Grandmont, 1978; Caplin and Nalebuff, 1988) and social learning (Kartik et al., 2024a).

An alternative notion—generally weaker and equivalent only in one dimension—is connected choice: the set of types that find an action (uniquely) optimal is connected. Subject to some regularity conditions, connected choice characterizes when local IC is sufficient on the full type space (for mechanisms with finite range; Proposition 2), rather than on every line segment.

We find convex choice more appealing for three related reasons. First, it ensures that IC between any two types can be verified via local IC along the line segment joining them; this is more tractable than checking local IC along all paths connecting the two types. Indeed, our proof of Proposition 3 relies on convex choice. Second, convex choice sets are more likely to be economically meaningful; for instance, in multidimensional cheap talk, equilibria that merely partition the sender’s type space into connected sets would be less satisfactory. Third, convex choice ties to directional single crossing and its consequences; connected choice has no such analog.

A.1 Proof of Proposition 1

  • Proof that statement 1 ⟹ statement 2..

    Assume convex choice and segment-thin indifferences. Fix an arbitrary line segment Θ′⊂Θ and let m:Θ′→A satisfy local IC. Fix any two distinct types in Θ′; we can label the line segment between these types as [0,1], corresponding to the convex combinations of the two types. Our goal is to show that u⁢(m⁢(0),0)≥u⁢(m⁢(1),0), which implies IC on Θ′.

    Local IC implies that each type θ∈[0,1] has a neighborhood Nθ⊂[0,1] satisfying condition (LIC). For each θ, there is an open ball Bθ⊂Nθ centered at θ. These balls form an open cover of [0,1]; as [0,1] is compact, there is a finite subcover {Bθi}i=1K. We assume without loss that this subcover is minimal and its indices satisfy θ1<…<θK.

    Then 0∈Bθ1 and 1∈BθK. Local IC implies

    u⁢(m⁢(0),0)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ1),0). (2)

    Now choose any θ~1∈Bθ1∩Bθ2 with θ1<θ~1<θ2. By local IC,

    u⁢(m⁢(θ1),θ1)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ~1),θ1), (3)
    u⁢(m⁢(θ~1),θ~1)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ1),θ~1). (4)

    We claim that

    u⁢(m⁢(θ1),0)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ~1),0). (5)

    To prove inequality (5), suppose to the contrary u⁢(m⁢(θ~1),0)>u⁢(m⁢(θ1),0). Then inequality (3) implies θ1>0. If inequality (4) holds strictly, then convex choice implies u⁢(m⁢(θ~1),θ1)>u⁢(m⁢(θ1),θ1), contradicting (3). If (4) holds with equality, then since θ1∈(0,θ~1), it follows from segment-thin indifferences and convex choice that u⁢(m⁢(θ~1),θ1)>u⁢(m⁢(θ1),θ1), contradicting (3).

    Combining (2) and (5), u⁢(m⁢(0),0)≥u⁢(m⁢(θ~1),0). Continuing in the same fashion, we obtain u⁢(m⁢(0),0)≥u⁢(m⁢(1),0), as desired. ∎

  • Proof that statement 1 ⟹ statement 2..

    Fix a locally IC mechanism m with finite range and suppose toward contradiction that m is not IC. First, replace every action in the range by a fixed representative of its payoff-equivalence class (where a∼a′ if u⁢(a,⋅)=u⁢(a′,⋅)). This changes no utility comparison, so it preserves local IC—with the same neighborhoods—and the failure of IC. We may thus assume that the range R:=m⁢(Θ) contains no two payoff-equivalent actions.

    Second, we claim that types with a unique maximizer in R are dense in Θ. For distinct b,c∈R, the tie set Tb⁢c:={θ:u⁢(b,θ)=u⁢(c,θ)} is closed, being the complement of the union of two open strict-preference sets, and it has empty interior: since b and c are not payoff-equivalent, one of Sb{b,c} and Sc{b,c} is nonempty, say the former, and thin indifferences yields Tb⁢c⊂Wb{b,c}⊂clΘ⁡Sb{b,c}; a nonempty open subset of Tb⁢c would then have to intersect Sb{b,c}, which is disjoint from Tb⁢c. As a finite union of closed sets with empty interior has empty interior, the set G:=Θ∖⋃b≠c∈RTb⁢c is open and dense, and every type in G strictly ranks all elements of R.

    Third, we identify a type with strict preferences and an incentive to misreport. Since m is not IC, there is θ^ with u⁢(m⁢(θ^),θ^)<maxr∈R⁡u⁢(r,θ^); let M be the set of maximizers of u⁢(⋅,θ^) in R, and fix b′∈M. The set Sb′{b′}∪(R∖M) of types that strictly prefer b′ to every element of R∖M contains θ^ by the definition of M, and it is open, being a finite intersection of strict-preference sets. Hence its intersection with Nθ^ contains some θ0∈G. Local IC at θ^ implies m⁢(θ0)∉M; hence u⁢(b′,θ0)>u⁢(m⁢(θ0),θ0). Since θ0∈G, some b∈R is uniquely optimal in R at θ0, and u⁢(b,θ0)≥u⁢(b′,θ0)>u⁢(m⁢(θ0),θ0). To summarize: type θ0 strictly prefers b to every other alternative in R, i.e., θ0∈SbR, but m⁢(θ0)≠b.

    We will derive a contradiction by enlarging a choice set on which some type receives b and finds it uniquely optimal. Let A′⊂R be a maximal set containing b such that b∈m⁢(SbA′). Such a set exists because A′={b} satisfies this condition and R is finite. Note that θ0∈SbR⊂SbA′. By connected choice, SbA′ is connected. It contains a type assigned b and a type assigned another action, namely θ0. Hence, there are θ1,θ′∈SbA′ with m⁢(θ1)=b≠m⁢(θ′) such that θ′∈Nθ1 or θ1∈Nθ′. Otherwise, {θ∈SbA′:m⁢(θ)=b} and {θ∈SbA′:m⁢(θ)≠b} would each contain Nθ∩SbA′ for each of their members θ, and thus partition SbA′ into two nonempty, relatively open sets. Let c:=m⁢(θ′). By thin indifferences, there is then some θ2∈Nθ1∩SbA′′. Local IC at θ1 gives u⁢(m⁢(θ2),θ2)≥u⁢(b,θ2), and since m⁢(θ2)∈A′′, we get m⁢(θ2)=b. As θ2∈SbA′∪{c}, we obtain b∈m⁢(SbA′∪{c}), contradicting the maximality of A′, since c∉A′. Thus m is IC. ∎

A.3 Proof of Remark 1

Neither condition implies the other.

For segment-thin without thin indifferences, let Θ=[0,1], A={a,b,c}, u⁢(a,⋅)=0, u⁢(b,θ)=−2⁢θ, and u⁢(c,θ)=1 if θ∈(0,1/2] while u⁢(c,θ)=−1 otherwise. Segment-thin indifferences holds because the only indifference is between a and b at θ=0, and u⁢(a,⋅)>u⁢(b,⋅) on (0,1]. Thin indifferences fails because 0∈WaA, yet SaA=(1/2,1], whose closure excludes 0.

For thin without segment-thin indifferences, let Θ=ℝ2, A={a,b}, u⁢(a,θ)=θ(1)2−θ(2), and u⁢(b,⋅)=0. Thin indifferences holds because every indifferent type (t,t2) is the limit of the types (t,t2±ε), which strictly prefer b and a, respectively. Segment-thin indifferences fails because θ′=(−3,4) strictly prefers a and θ′′=(2,4) is indifferent, yet all types (x,4) with |x|<2 strictly prefer b.

Under the openness assumption, segment-thin indifferences implies thin indifferences.

Assume every strict-preference set is open and u has segment-thin indifferences. Fix a finite choice set B, a∈B with some η∈SaB, and θ0∈WaB. If θ0=η there is nothing to prove, so assume θ0≠η. Parametrize ℓ⁢(η,θ0) by [0,1] and, for b∈B∖{a}, let fb:=u⁢(a,⋅)−u⁢(b,⋅) along this segment. Each fb satisfies fb⁢(0)>0 and fb⁢(1)≥0, and the sets {fb>0} and {fb<0} are open in [0,1]. We claim fb≥0 on [0,1]. If fb⁢(s)<0 for some s, let q:=inf{t≥s:fb⁢(t)≥0}; openness of the two strict sets implies fb<0 on [s,q) and fb⁢(q)=0. But segment-thin indifferences applied to the pair at parameters 0 and q requires points at which a is strictly preferred to b accumulating at q from within [0,q], and there are none in [s,q)—a contradiction. Next, {fb>0} is open and dense in [0,1]: every zero of fb is a limit of strict points, by segment-thin indifferences applied to the pair at parameters 0 and that zero. A finite intersection of open dense subsets of [0,1] is dense, so points at which a is strictly preferred to every b∈B∖{a} accumulate at 1. Such points lie in SaB, whence θ0∈clΘ⁡SaB.

Given convex choice, segment-thin indifferences holds if and only if thin indifferences holds on every line segment.

For a line segment ℓ⊂Θ and B⊂A, a∈B, write SaB,ℓ and WaB,ℓ for the sets from Section 2 defined relative to ℓ, with closures also taken in ℓ. If segment-thin indifferences fails for some a′,a′′ and θ′,θ′′, let ℓ:=ℓ⁢(θ′,θ′′) and B:={a′,a′′}. Then θ′∈Sa′B,ℓ and θ′′∈Wa′B,ℓ, while a sequence in Sa′B,ℓ converging to θ′′ is precisely what the failure denies; so thin indifferences fails on ℓ.

Conversely, suppose u has convex choice and segment-thin indifferences. Fix a segment ℓ, a finite choice set B, a∈B with some η∈SaB,ℓ, and any θ0∈WaB,ℓ. If θ0=η there is nothing to prove, so assume θ0≠η. It suffices to show that a is strictly preferred to each b∈B∖{a} at every point strictly between η and θ0; the open segment between η and θ0 then lies in SaB,ℓ, whence θ0∈clℓ⁡SaB,ℓ. If u⁢(a,θ0)>u⁢(b,θ0), this follows from the convexity of Sa{a,b}, which contains η and θ0. If u⁢(a,θ0)=u⁢(b,θ0), segment-thin indifferences supplies types θn∈ℓ⁢(η,θ0) converging to θ0 with u⁢(a,θn)>u⁢(b,θn). Any point strictly between η and θ0 lies in ℓ⁢(η,θn) for large n, so the convexity of Sa{a,b}, which contains η and θn, yields the conclusion. ∎

A.4 Proof of Proposition 3

Let υ:Θ→Y be a weakly monotone allocation rule with finite range. Since Θ⊂ℝn is convex and u~⁢(y,θ) is continuous in θ, Theorem 5 in Berger et al. (2017, p. 380) implies that it is sufficient to show that υ is “line implementable” (Berger et al., 2017, p. 373).

Towards contradiction, suppose there is a line segment such that the restriction of υ to this line segment is not implementable. We can label types on this line segment as [0,1] corresponding to the convex combinations of its end points. It follows from Rochet (1987) that cyclical monotonicity is violated: there is a finite set {θ1,…,θK}⊂[0,1] such that

∑i=1K[u~⁢(υ⁢(θi+1),θi)−u~⁢(υ⁢(θi),θi)]>0, (6)

where θK+1:=θ1.

For y,y′∈υ⁢(Θ), let D~y,y′ denote the restriction of u~⁢(y,⋅)−u~⁢(y′,⋅) to [0,1], which is monotonic by convex choice (as noted after Proposition 3). Together with D~y,y′′=D~y,y′+D~y′,y′′, monotonicity implies that the relation “D~y,y′ is increasing” on the finite set υ⁢(Θ) is complete and transitive, so we can enumerate υ⁢(Θ)={y1,…,yJ} such that D~yk,yl is increasing whenever k>l.

We claim that if υ⁢(θ)=yk and υ⁢(θ′)=yl with k>l, then D~yj,yj−1⁢(θ)≥D~yj,yj−1⁢(θ′) for all j∈{l+1,…,k}. The claim is immediate if θ≥θ′. If θ<θ′, then weak monotonicity gives D~yk,yl⁢(θ)≥D~yk,yl⁢(θ′), so the increasing function D~yk,yl=∑j=l+1kD~yj,yj−1 is constant on [θ,θ′], and hence so is each of its increasing summands.

Now let ki be the index with υ⁢(θi)=yki, so that kK+1=k1. The left-hand side of (6) equals

−∑i=1KD~yki,yki+1⁢(θi)=−∑j=2J[∑i:ki≥jD~yj,yj−1⁢(θi)−∑i:ki+1≥jD~yj,yj−1⁢(θi)],

where the equality follows from D~yk,yl=∑j=2kD~yj,yj−1−∑j=2lD~yj,yj−1 by exchanging the order of summation. Fix j. Since kK+1=k1, the two sums over i inside the display’s bracket have equally many terms. The terms of the second sum with ki≥j also appear in the first sum. By the claim in the previous paragraph, every term D~yj,yj−1⁢(θi) with ki≥j is at least as large as every term with ki<j, so the first sum is at least the second. Hence the left-hand side of (6) is at most 0, a contradiction. Q.E.D.

Definitions.

A.7 Proof of Proposition 5

For this proof, we fix an arbitrary action a∗∈A and work with the function

f⁢(a,θ):=u⁢(a,θ)−u⁢(a∗,θ).

Since we are interested in representations of u, we say that a function f~ is a representative of f if there is a representation u~ of u such that f~⁢(a,θ)=u~⁢(a,θ)−u~⁢(a∗,θ). Note that any type-dependent positive linear transformation of f (i.e., g⁢(θ)⁢f⁢(a,θ) with g⁢(⋅)>0) is a representative of f. We say that f is DSC-preserving if for all finite index sets I, {ai}i∈I⊂A, and {λi}i∈I⊂ℝ, it holds that ∑i∈Iλi⁢f⁢(ai,⋅) is DSC.

An affine representation implies DSCD because affine utility differences are DSC and representations preserve their signs. For the other implication, assume DSCD.

Let A∗:=A∖{a∗}. Analogous to Kartik et al. (2024b, proof of Theorem 2), for every μ:A∗→ℝ with finite support Aμ, there exist probability distributions P,Q on Aμ∪{a∗} such that ∑a∈Aμf⁢(a,θ)⁢μ⁢(a) is a multiple of

Proposition A.1.

Assume Θ=ℝn and f⁢(a,θ) is DSC-preserving and differentiable in θ. If no type is totally indifferent, then f is either one-dimensional or has an affine representative.

Lemma A.1.

Let V be a vector space of differentiable functions on a convex set Θ, all of which are DSC. If linearly independent f0,f1∈V vanish at θ0∈ri⁡Θ and ∇f0⁢(θ0)≠0, then there is β:Θ→(0,∞) such that β⁢g is affine for every g∈V.

    g⁢(θ)⁢∇f0⁢(z)⋅(θ−z)=f0⁢(θ)⁢∇g⁢(z)⋅(θ−z)for every ⁢θ∈Θ. (8)

    fi⁢(θ0+t⁢(θ−θ0))=t⁢∇fi⁢(θ0)⋅(θ−θ0)+o⁢(t)>0

    f~1⁢(θ):=∇f1⁢(θ0)⋅(θ−θ0),

    g~⁢(θ):=f~1⁢(θ)f1⁢(θ)⁢g⁢(θ)where ⁢f1⁢(θ)≠0. (10)

    The multiplier is positive by (9). Equation (8) at θ0, with g=f1, gives

Example B.5.

C.1 On Grandmont’s (1978) Characterization

In this subsection, we elaborate on the connection between Proposition 4 and Grandmont (1978). It is useful to begin with a strengthening of DSCD.

Definition C.1.

A function f:Θ→ℝ is strictly directionally single crossing (strict DSC) if it is DSC in some direction α and, in addition, either f⁢(⋅)=0 or for all θ,θ′∈Θ,

[(θ−θ′)⋅α>0⁢ and ⁢f⁢(θ′)=0]⟹f⁢(θ)≠0.

The utility function u:A×Θ→ℝ has strict directionally single-crossing differences (strict DSCD) if for all a,a′∈A, the difference u⁢(a,θ)−u⁢(a′,θ) is strictly DSC.

Strict DSC can be interpreted as requiring that there is a hyperplane such that the sets {θ:f⁢(θ)<0} and {θ:f⁢(θ)>0} are either empty, full, or intersections of Θ with half-spaces defined by that hyperplane, and {θ:f⁢(θ)=0} is either empty, full, or the intersection of Θ with that hyperplane. So 2(a) satisfies strict DSC whereas 2(b) violates it.

As explained subsequently, the following result follows from Grandmont (1978).

Proposition C.1.

Assume Θ is open and that

∀a,a′∈A, the set {θ:u⁢(a,θ)≥u⁢(a′,θ)} is closed in Θ. (13)

Then u has convex choice and segment-thin indifferences if and only if it has strict DSCD.

Hence, under Proposition C.1’s two assumptions, convex choice and segment-thin indifferences are fully characterized by strict DSCD.242424Strict DSCD implies convex choice and segment-thin indifferences without either assumption. But absent either assumption, strict DSCD can fail despite convex choice and segment-thin indifferences. Here is an example in which Θ is not open: Θ=[0,1], A={a′,a′′}, u⁢(a′,⋅)=0, and u⁢(a′′,θ)=0 if θ∈{0,1} and u⁢(a′′,θ)>0 otherwise. For an example absent (13), consider the left figure in Example B.3. By comparison, Proposition 4 required neither assumption and showed that convex choice alone is “almost” characterized by just DSCD.

To tie Proposition C.1 to Grandmont (1978), say that u has convex weak choice if for all a,a′∈A, the set {θ:u⁢(a,θ)≥u⁢(a′,θ)} is convex. In our terminology, Grandmont’s condition (H.2) is the conjunction of convex weak choice, convex choice, and segment-thin indifferences.252525The one-dimensional example in footnote 24 has convex choice and segment-thin indifferences but not convex weak choice. Note that given convex choice and segment-thin indifferences, convex weak choice is equivalent to convex indifference, i.e., for all a,a′, the set {θ:u⁢(a,θ)=u⁢(a′,θ)} is convex.
